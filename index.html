Welcome to the inaugural issue of the Piedmont Triad Economic Activity Report. I started this because our region’s biggest moments, like JetZero’s arrival, deserve more than a one-time headline. Each month, this report distills four labor-market indicators into a single, consistent signal of whether the Triad’s momentum is building or fading, so you don’t have to wait for the next big announcement to know how the region is actually doing.

Reading the Index

What the numbers mean

1

Index Level: 108.3

The Triad is 8.3 points above its 2019 baseline and 5.6 points above the 2007 peak of 102.7, the highest reading in the index’s 19-year history.

How to read this

At 108.3 (2019 = 100), the Triad’s economy has not only fully recovered from the COVID-19 shock but has expanded meaningfully beyond its pre-pandemic level. The current reading also surpasses the pre-recession peak recorded in mid-2007. For historical perspective: after the 2008–09 recession, the region spent roughly six years working its way back to its pre-crisis level before eventually surpassing it. The current post-pandemic expansion has been faster and, by this measure, more complete.

A reading above 100 means the combined signal from employment, hours, and earnings across the three MSAs is running ahead of where it stood before COVID-19; a reading below 100 would indicate an incomplete recovery or a subsequent pullback. The level chart places the current reading in the full sweep of history from 2007 onward, the Great Recession trough, the long post-2009 climb, the sharp but brief COVID contraction of 2020, and the expansion that followed.

2

Growth Momentum: 71 out of 100

Momentum is above the 50-point neutral threshold, indicating that recent growth is running faster than the region’s historical norm.

How to read this

If the index level is the scoreboard, growth momentum is the speedometer. It measures not where the Triad economy currently stands, but how fast it is moving, and whether that pace is building or fading relative to the region’s own history. The score is built from the 3-month average growth rate of the index, on a 0–100 scale: 50 is neutral, above 50 signals above-average growth, and below 50 signals growth has slowed relative to historical norms.

Momentum is a forward-leaning indicator. A drop toward or below 50 would be an early warning that the expansion is losing steam, even while the headline level remains strong.

3

Labor Utilization: 98.1

Average weekly hours are below the 2019 baseline of 100.

How to read this

Labor utilization measures average weekly hours worked per employee, indexed so that the 2019 average equals 100. It captures the intensity with which the existing workforce is being deployed. Employers routinely adjust hours before they adjust headcount, so labor utilization is typically one of the first places where changes in the economic climate show up.

At 98.1, workers are logging fewer hours than before the pandemic, pointing to slack in labor demand that the headline index may not fully capture. The 1.9-point gap from the 100 baseline is modest and within the normal range of historical variation, but its persistence alongside a headline index above 100 is worth noting. One reading is that Triad employers still have room to intensify the use of their current workforce before needing to add significant new positions, a pattern that, if demand continues to grow, could precede a new wave of hiring.

About
What is the Piedmont Triad Economic Activity Report?

The Piedmont Triad, the Greensboro–High Point, Winston–Salem, and Burlington metropolitan areas, is one of North Carolina’s largest and most economically dynamic regions. Over three decades it has moved away from its legacy base in furniture, textiles, and tobacco toward a more diversified economy anchored in healthcare, logistics, advanced manufacturing, defense, and financial services. Tracking that evolution requires more than any single data series can provide.

The report combines four labor market indicators from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics payroll employment, unemployment, average weekly hours, and average hourly earnings, across all three metropolitan areas into a single Economic Activity Index. Principal component analysis distills them into one monthly number that captures their common signal. The data are seasonally adjusted, calibrated so that movements in the index correspond to regional employment growth, and rebased so that the 2019 average equals 100.

Methodology and data

Methodology: Composite index of employment, unemployment, average weekly hours, and average hourly earnings across the Greensboro–High Point, Winston–Salem, and Burlington MSAs. Constructed via principal component analysis on seasonally adjusted BLS series (X-13-ARIMA-SEATS), calibrated to employment growth via OLS, and rebased to 2019 = 100. Index readings are preliminary and subject to revision as BLS revises the underlying CES and LAUS series; figures here reflect data as of July 2026. Data: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Local Area Unemployment Statistics and Current Employment Statistics programs.
